# Gloups can serve a million users for 4 cents each

The cheapest stack that takes Gloups to 1M MAU keeps most of the proposed direction: NestJS (Fastify adapter, Node 24 LTS) in containers under Coolify on Hetzner, PostgreSQL in the EU (managed at first, self-hosted once volume justifies it), Drizzle, Better Auth inside NestJS, Expo SDK 58 with local SQLite and an idempotent command queue, Expo push, PostHog EU, RevenueCat, and a small LLM served from an EU region in a nightly batch. Gloups starts from zero, with no existing users, code or vendor accounts to carry over, so per-user billing can be avoided from day one: Clerk would cost about $1,000 a month at 100k MRU and about $17,000 at 1M, and EAS Update's per-MAU billing $2,000 to $4,000 at 1M, which a self-hosted update server removes once volume justifies it. By our estimates, the monthly bill goes from about 50-120 at 1k MAU to 290-560 at 10k, 2,800-6,900 at 100k and 25,000-67,000 at 1M (USD and EUR treated at parity). The top of each range assumes the paid services are kept by default. At scale, the LLM coach is 60 to 80% of the optimized total, while application infrastructure stays a minor line at every tier. On compliance, GDPR Article 9, the store rules and US law all require the same architecture: health data in the EU behind an internal ID, with US vendors limited to identity and billing. French HDS hosting is probably not required as long as no care pathway exists. All prices were seen on 27 October 2026 unless stated otherwise. Usage volumes (30% DAU/MAU, 50 API calls per DAU per day, tokens per request) are unmeasured assumptions, to be replaced with real data from launch.

## Starting from zero changes four proposed choices

The research below was first framed around reusing existing applications and vendor accounts. There are none: every choice is made on cost, simplicity and speed alone. That removes the arguments for four proposals.

| Proposed choice                     | From-scratch verdict      | Reason                                                                                                                           |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Clerk                               | Better Auth inside NestJS | No users to migrate, no license fee at any tier, identity stays in the EU Postgres                                               |
| Prisma                              | Drizzle                   | No code to reuse; avoids the certain Prisma 7 to 8 client rewrite and keeps time-based aggregates close to SQL                   |
| Bun + Nx                            | Bun workspaces only       | Two apps and one contracts package do not need a task graph or remote cache; Nx can be added if the repository grows             |
| Self-hosted PostgreSQL from day one | Scaleway managed at first | About EUR 17-32 a month with an HA node, close to a Hetzner server, without owning backups, restore and failover for health data |

NestJS, Expo, Coolify on Hetzner for the stateless API, PostHog EU and RevenueCat hold up without any reuse argument. The sections below keep the sourced facts as gathered; the recommendations, cost table and thresholds reflect these verdicts.

## Servers are cheap: Hetzner under Coolify beats managed platforms by 2 to 8 times

### Compute: the database and the LLM cost money, NestJS does not

Hetzner remains the cheapest EU capacity found. **A 4 vCPU / 8 GB machine (CPX32) costs EUR 35.99 a month excl. VAT with 20 TB of traffic included**, and extra traffic costs EUR 1/TB in the EU ([Hetzner Regular Performance](https://www.hetzner.com/cloud/regular-performance/)). For comparable always-on capacity, Railway bills about $20 per vCPU-month and $10 per GB-month of RAM, with egress at $0.05/GB ([Railway pricing](https://railway.com/pricing)). Fly.io lists $7.73 for a 1 GB shared-cpu-1x in Frankfurt ([Fly.io pricing](https://docs.fly.io/about/pricing)). By our estimate, a 4 vCPU / 8 GB machine therefore costs EUR 36 at Hetzner versus about $160 on Railway. Our load estimate at 1M MAU (30% DAU, 50 calls per day, evening peak at 5x the average) gives about 175 requests/s on average and 900 at peak, which is 2 to 8 vCPUs of NestJS: the application server is not the dominant cost at any tier. Coolify provides deploys, TLS and scheduled dumps. It provides no point-in-time recovery (PITR), no automatic failover and no autoscaling. The main risk of this path is therefore a single-host outage, not price.

On the runtime, **Fastify serves about 88,000 requests/s versus about 60,000 for Express 5.2.1 in the official benchmark updated on 17 August 2026** ([Fastify benchmarks](https://www.fastify.dev/benchmarks/)). The real gap inside NestJS is only indicative, from posts with unclear methodology ([tech-insider.org](https://tech-insider.org/?p=22176)). The adapter must be chosen on day one, because Express-only middleware needs Fastify equivalents. Bun is moving fast: 1.4 shipped on 20 August 2026 with Node.js 26.3.0 compatibility, and 1.4.4 on 22 October ([Bun blog](https://bun.com/blog)). However, Prisma's official ORM 8 guide for NestJS was tested on NestJS 11 with Node 24.11+ or 22.18+ and does not mention Bun ([Prisma blog](https://www.prisma.io/blog/prisma-orm-8-nestjs)). 2026 third-party guides advise keeping Bun for installs and CI and Node for high-traffic production servers ([alexcloudstar.com](https://alexcloudstar.com/blog/bun-compatibility-2026-npm-nodejs-nextjs/)). That split stays compatible with the repository rule (Bun 1.4.2 as package manager). Making Bun the production runtime first requires a load test on the real application.

Data volume decides. Our estimate: about 15 KB per active user per day (meals, sets, sleep stages), or 10-12 MB a year with indexes. With 50% of MAU actively logging, that gives on the order of **5-6 GB at 1k MAU, 55 GB at 10k, 550 GB at 100k and 3 to 5.5 TB at 1M**. At that last tier, Neon storage alone would cost $1,000 to $1,900 a month before compute and PITR, while Scaleway local storage stays in the tens of euros. Storing raw minute-level HealthKit samples (heart rate, steps) would multiply that volume by 10 or more. The architectural rule that follows is to store daily aggregates and keep raw samples only where coaching needs them, with retention. At these volumes, native monthly range partitioning on the few heavy tables is enough. TimescaleDB is only justified for raw series, and its availability on managed offers was not verified.

### ORM, job queues and object storage

**Prisma 7.0.0 (19 November 2025) made the Rust-free client, compiled to WebAssembly, the default** ([Prisma changelog](https://www.prisma.io/changelog.md)), removing the old engine binary. Prisma ORM 8 was still a release candidate on 2 October 2026. Its API changes deeply: the client is a plain object, `$extends` is removed, queries end with `.all()`/`.first()` ([Prisma blog](https://www.prisma.io/blog/prisma-orm-8-nestjs)). Starting on Prisma 7.x with `@prisma/adapter-pg` therefore means a rewrite later. Starting on the 8 RC conflicts with the expectation of stable dependencies. Drizzle or Kysely would avoid that churn, at the cost of Prisma Migrate's ergonomics. No independent 2026 benchmark settles the choice between these ORMs. With no existing Prisma code to reuse, Drizzle avoids the 7 to 8 rewrite, keeps the hot aggregates (day summary, workout history) in typed SQL-like queries and generates reviewable SQL migrations.

For background work (nightly briefings, health imports, exports, notifications), our estimate at 1M MAU is 10 to 25 jobs/s. A Postgres-backed queue (`SKIP LOCKED`, such as pg-boss or Graphile Worker) absorbs that without adding a stateful service. No source was gathered for these libraries: this is an inference to validate. For media, **Cloudflare R2 charges $0.015/GB-month with free egress** ([Cloudflare R2](https://developers.cloudflare.com/r2/pricing/)), but the pricing page does not mention an EU jurisdiction. Hetzner Object Storage costs EUR 6.49 a month for 1 TB stored and 1 TB egress, and had a degradation on 2 July 2026 ([Sliplane](https://sliplane.io/blog/cheap-object-storage-providers-europe), a competing provider, to verify). Meal photos can reveal health data: until R2's EU jurisdiction is confirmed, Hetzner storage in the EU is the default for both backups and media, served through short-lived presigned URLs.

## Expo covers health, timers and offline without a paid sync vendor

**Expo SDK 58 (21 October 2026) targets iOS 27 and React Native 0.88**, with stabilized native tabs and the Liquid Glass header by default ([Expo SDK 58](https://expo.dev/blog/how-to-upgrade-to-sdk-58)). Since SDK 56, Expo UI exposes SwiftUI and Jetpack Compose as stable APIs, and iOS Widgets and Live Activities are stable too ([Expo SDK 56](https://expo.dev/changelog/sdk-56)). That meets the goal of native components per platform without maintaining two UI codebases. Flutter draws its own widgets. KMP/Compose Multiplatform forces Kotlin and Swift on a TypeScript team that already shares types with NestJS. Fully native doubles the UI work. No source showed a cost advantage for any of these alternatives. Expo has a real cost: its pace of 3 to 4 SDKs a year brings breaking changes and sometimes regressions. SDK 56 had a Hermes v1 memory regression, for example, fixed in 57 ([Expo SDK 57](https://expo.dev/changelog/sdk-57)). By our estimate, plan 1 to 3 developer-days per upgrade.

For health data, `@kingstinct/react-native-healthkit` (Nitro modules, Expo plugin with a `background` option, sleep and 75+ workout types) is the most active iOS option ([GitHub kingstinct](https://github.com/kingstinct/react-native-healthkit)). On Android, `react-native-health-connect` v4 includes its Expo plugin and requires compileSdk and targetSdk 36 ([GitHub matinzd](https://github.com/matinzd/react-native-health-connect)). Two platform constraints shape the design. On iOS, observer queries stop firing after the user force-quits the app ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/695073)). On Android, background reads and history beyond 30 days need dedicated permissions ([Android, Read raw data](https://developer.android.com/health-and-fitness/health-connect/read-data)). Health sync is therefore always best effort, with a foreground catch-up on every app open. Menstrual cycle permissions should be requested only when the user enables the feature.

Rest timers must not depend on JavaScript running. The app stores an absolute deadline (`restEndsAt`) in SQLite, schedules a backup local notification at that time, and lets the system render the countdown. On iOS, `expo-widgets` starts, updates and ends a Live Activity locally, without push ([Expo Widgets](https://docs.expo.dev/versions/latest/sdk/widgets/)). On Android, the right mechanism is a foreground service of type `health`, while the `shortService` type is capped at about 3 minutes ([Android FGS types](https://developer.android.com/develop/background-work/services/fgs/service-types)). A small Expo module in Kotlin is enough.

For offline, PowerSync is the strongest managed alternative (Pro from $49 a month, self-hosted Open Edition free) ([PowerSync pricing](https://powersync.com/pricing.md)). Electric now bills $1 per million writes ([Electric](https://electric-sql.com/blog/2026/03/24/electric-cloud-pricing)). Gloups data is mostly private, append-only, with few conflicts between devices. The cheapest solution is therefore local `expo-sqlite`, a queue of commands with client-generated UUIDs and a server-side idempotency key, and a cursor-based pull. It costs no subscription, takes by our estimate 1 to 3 weeks to build, keeps data in our EU Postgres and meets the requirement to retain retry identity. For notifications, **Expo Push is free with a limit of 600 notifications/s per project** ([Expo push FAQ](https://docs.expo.dev/push-notifications/faq/)). Keeping the native tokens too allows a later move to direct APNs/FCM without a mobile release. Finally, Hermes ships `Intl` on both platforms ([React Native 0.65](https://reactnative.dev/blog/2021/08/17/version-065)), enough to format units and dates without polyfills.

## The LLM coach costs 1 to 3 cents per MAU when output, batch and region are constrained

Entry-level model prices have collapsed. **Claude Haiku 5.5 costs $0.10 input and $0.50 output per million tokens, $0.01 for cache reads and half price in batch** ([Claude pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). gpt-5-nano is at $0.05/$0.40 ([OpenAI pricing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing)) and Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite at $0.10/$0.40 ([Gemini pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing)). Under our assumptions (2,000-token shared cached prefix, 1,500 tokens of per-user facts, 400 output tokens, 30 batched briefings and 20 chat turns per MAU per month), the monthly cost per MAU comes out as follows:

| Model                                              | Estimated cost per MAU-month       | Estimate at 1M MAU |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ------------------ |
| gpt-5-nano                                         | about $0.008                       | about $8k          |
| Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite                              | about $0.012                       | about $12k         |
| Claude Haiku 5.5 (+30% tokenizer, +10% Bedrock EU) | $0.013 to $0.018                   | $13k to $19k       |
| Scaleway gpt-oss-120b (no cache or batch)          | about EUR 0.041                    | about EUR 41k      |
| Gemini 3.6 Flash                                   | $0.095, then $0.19 from 2027-01-01 | $95k to $190k      |
| Claude Sonnet 5.5                                  | about $0.24                        | about $244k        |

Two notes on these figures. The tokenizer of Claude models since 4.7 produces about 30% more tokens for the same text ([Claude pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)), so compare cost per request, not per token. And Gemini 3.6/3.8 Flash prices double on 1 January 2027 ([Gemini pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing)). Routing 95% of calls to Haiku 5.5 and 5% to Sonnet 5.5 comes to about $0.025 per MAU. The real budget risk is chat: by our estimate, a heavy user at 300 turns a month costs $0.11 on Haiku 5.5 but about $2 on Sonnet 5.5. Per-plan quotas are therefore necessary. Meal photos are the second variable cost: about $0.0004 per photo by our estimate, or $0.04 a month for a user who sends 3 a day, more than all of their text coaching. Calories always come from the nutrition database; the model only proposes foods and portions.

The region constraint changes the ranking more than price does. **Anthropic's first-party API offers only `global` and `us` as inference geography** ([Anthropic data residency](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/manage-claude/data-residency)). EU-processed Claude therefore goes through Bedrock's EU geographic profile (Frankfurt, Ireland, Paris) ([AWS Haiku 5.5 model card](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/model-card-anthropic-claude-haiku-5-5.html)), at a 10% premium. OpenAI also charges 10% more for regional processing ([OpenAI pricing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing)), and its EU residency requires zero data retention or approved modified abuse monitoring ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/06/openai-launches-data-residency-in-europe)). Vertex processes requests in the requested region, but zero retention there requires an exception to abuse-monitoring logging ([Vertex data governance](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/data-governance)). Mistral and Scaleway are EU by default, but their retention terms appear only in conflicting secondary sources ([char.com](https://char.com/blog/mistral-data-retention-policy)). The rational choice is a provider port in NestJS and a small EU-processed model by default (Haiku 5.5 on Bedrock EU, or gpt-5-nano with EU residency), decided by a French-quality test. Escalation to a mid-tier model must be explicit. In order of impact: deterministic templates for the numbers, the nightly batch limited to users active in the last 7 days, a shared cached prefix, short structured outputs, then chat quotas. At 1M MAU, per-event LLM observability would cost as much as the tokens: 50M generations at PostHog's lowest band is more than $3,200 a month ([PostHog AI observability](https://posthog.com/ai-observability/pricing)). Traces must therefore be sampled, or Langfuse self-hosted, whose open-source edition has no usage fee ([Langfuse](https://langfuse.com/resources/engineering/clarifications)).

## Beyond 50k users, third-party services cost more than infrastructure

**Clerk now bills per MRU (a user who returns at least one day after sign-up): free up to 50,000 per app, then $0.02 up to 100k and $0.018 up to 1M** ([Clerk pricing](https://clerk.com/pricing)). In the worst case where MRU equals MAU, that is about $1,025 a month at 100k and $17,225 at 1M. The same page says nothing about EU residency and reserves the HIPAA BAA for Enterprise. Better Auth, a self-hosted TypeScript library on our own Postgres, has no license fee ([Better Auth](https://www.better-auth.com/)). Its real cost is engineering: security patches, rate limiting, account recovery. Supabase Auth would cost about $2,950 at 1M ([Toolradar](https://toolradar.com/blog/supabase-pricing-2026)), but only makes sense if Supabase also hosts the database. Logto Cloud advertises "unlimited MAU" but bills access tokens ([Logto pricing](https://logto.io/pricing)): by our estimate, about $24,000 at 1M if each MAU issues 30 tokens a month. Starting from zero, Better Auth from day one avoids both the per-MRU bill and a later account migration. It needs Sign in with Apple whenever Google sign-in is offered, and the maturity of its NestJS and Expo integrations should be checked before adoption.

The other services behave better. PostHog's free tier (1M events a month) covers about 10k MAU at 100 events per MAU. Beyond that, per-band prices are only confirmed by conflicting third-party sources ([Costbench](https://costbench.com/software/product-analytics/posthog/)), and the official page could not be read. By our estimate, expect $325 to $940 at 100k MAU and $2,600 to $5,300 at 1M. Feature flags evaluated on every session could add about $1,900 at 1M unless they are cached on the device or evaluated locally in NestJS. RevenueCat is free up to $2,500 of tracked revenue, then takes 1% ([RevenueCat pricing](https://www.revenuecat.com/pricing/)). Qonversion takes 0.8% above $7,000 ([Qonversion](https://qonversion.io/pricing)). With 3% paying users at $5 net, we estimate about $1,500 a month at 1M MAU, far less than maintaining receipt validation in-house. For email, Amazon SES costs $0.10 per 1,000 sends ([SaaS Price Pulse](https://www.saaspricepulse.com/blog/amazon-ses-pricing-per-1000-emails-2026)) versus $650 per million at Resend ([Resend pricing](https://resend.com/pricing)). OneSignal, at $0.012 per mobile MAU ([OneSignal pricing](https://onesignal.com/pricing)), would cost $12,000 at 1M for what Expo Push does for free. For errors, Sentry's Developer plan (5k errors) then Team at $29 are enough up to 100k MAU with sampling ([Sentry pricing](https://sentry.io/pricing/)), and Grafana Cloud Free includes 50 GB of logs and traces ([Grafana pricing](https://grafana.com/pricing/)). The hidden bill is EAS: **EAS Update charges $0.005 per MAU beyond the plan quota, with Production at $199 for 50,000 MAU** ([Expo pricing](https://expo.dev/pricing)). Since the `expo-updates` protocol is open, a self-hosted server removes this line.

For the food database, the cheapest and most solid option in French and English is to import Open Food Facts, CIQUAL and USDA FoodData Central into our own Postgres. The public Open Food Facts API is limited to 15 reads/min and 10 searches/min per IP, and the project recommends importing the daily dumps ([OFF API docs](https://openfoodfacts.github.io/openfoodfacts-server/api/)). The ODbL license requires attribution and share-alike for derived databases ([OFF terms](https://world.openfoodfacts.org/terms-of-use)): user-entered foods must stay in a separate table, and the scope needs legal review. CIQUAL covers 3,185 generic foods ([ANSES CIQUAL](https://www.anses.fr/en/node/79557)). Commercial APIs lock down caching: Edamam starts at $14 for 100k calls but allows storing only a few fields and requires attribution ([Edamam](https://developer.edamam.com/food-database-api)). They therefore create a permanent per-call dependency. A white-label commercial API with worldwide coverage remains a fallback for barcodes outside Europe, at an unpublished price.

## Compliance requires three data zones, not HDS hosting

According to the CNIL, cross-referenced data counts as health data: **its own example is weight combined with step count or calorie intake** ([CNIL](https://www.cnil.fr/fr/quest-ce-ce-quune-donnee-de-sante)). Meals, body metrics, workouts, sleep, cycle and coach conversations therefore fall under GDPR Article 9 from day one. That requires explicit consent per purpose, a DPIA and records of processing, since the exemption for companies under 250 staff does not apply to Article 9 data ([GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)). HDS hosting is required for data collected during prevention, diagnosis, care or medico-social follow-up activities ([Legifrance via doctrine.fr](https://www.doctrine.fr/l/texts/codes/LEGITEXT000006072665/articles/LEGIARTI000006685779)). A French analysis reads these conditions as cumulative ([next.ink](https://next.ink/public-profile/?id=43883)). A wellness coach with no care pathway is therefore very likely out of scope, but no ANS or CNIL ruling confirms this for this specific case. Sharing data with a dietitian or a doctor from the app would likely trigger HDS.

Transfers to the United States remain legal but fragile. The EU General Court upheld the Data Privacy Framework on 3 September 2025, and appeal C-703/25 P is pending before the CJEU with no known date ([WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20251201-european-court-of-justice-to-review-challenge-to-eu-us-data-privacy-framework)). A competing, and therefore biased, source considers the framework weakened by a June 2026 US Supreme Court ruling ([sota.io](https://sota.io/blog/clerk-eu-alternative-gdpr-cloud-act-serverless-auth-2026)). In the US, the FTC explicitly uses a health app synced with a fitness tracker as an example, and **an unauthorized disclosure, for instance to a social media platform, counts as a breach that must be notified** ([FTC HBNR](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-ftcs-health-breach-notification-rule-0)). Washington's My Health My Data Act requires consent to share that is separate from consent to collect, and grants a private right of action ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2023/05/6-Things-to-Know-About-Washingtons-My-Health-My-Data-Law)). Apple forbids storing personal health information in iCloud and requires explicit permission before any sharing with third-party AI (guidelines 5.1.3(ii) and 5.1.2(i)) ([Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Google requires a Health apps declaration, a "not a medical device" statement and a justification for every Health Connect permission ([Google Play Health](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16679511)). The AI Act only requires telling users they are interacting with an AI (Article 50(1), applicable since 2 August 2026) ([Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/)), as long as the product avoids any diagnostic or treatment purpose that would qualify it as a medical device ([MDCG 2019-11](https://health.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2020-09/md_mdcg_2019_11_guidance_qualification_classification_software_en_0.pdf)).

All these rules lead to a single, inexpensive architecture. An identity zone (email, credentials, language, country) is held by Better Auth in its own EU schema; US vendors such as RevenueCat receive only an opaque ID and billing data. A health zone lives in Postgres in the EU, keyed by a random internal ID, with no name or email. An analytics zone, PostHog EU, receives only product events with no health content, with no autocapture on health screens and no advertising SDK. On top of that come an append-only consent ledger, with separate rows for collection and sharing, and application-level envelope encryption of the most sensitive fields (cycle, coach chat). Deleting a user's key then also erases their data in backups. The LLM pipeline assembles approved, pseudonymized facts on the server, then sends them to an EU zero-retention endpoint. It stores the output with the model and prompt version for audit. Almost none of these components carries a vendor cost. External items (DPIA, penetration test, legal opinion) could not be priced reliably and must come from quotes.

## Thresholds for switching from paid to self-hosted

**Database.** Scaleway managed with an HA node is the choice at launch: it costs about the same as a dedicated Hetzner server at small tiers and leaves backups, PITR and failover to the provider. Move to self-hosted Postgres with WAL archiving when managed storage or compute pricing exceeds the cost of owning restore and on-call, likely between 100 GB and 500 GB (10k to 100k MAU by our estimate). Add a replica with PgBouncer and a load balancer around 100k.

**Auth.** Better Auth runs inside NestJS from the start, so there is no auth threshold to watch.

**OTA.** A self-hosted `expo-updates` server is worth it once EAS Update exceeds the Production plan price, around 50k MAU. It becomes a priority before 200k MAU.

**Email.** Moving from Resend to SES in eu-west-3 or eu-central-1 is justified around 200k emails a month, about 100k MAU.

**Errors.** Moving to self-hosted GlitchTip or Better Stack's EU pricing makes sense when Sentry overage reaches a few hundred dollars, around 1M MAU.

**What not to switch.** Self-hosted PostHog is not justified before 1M MAU, because ClickHouse and Kafka cost more to operate than the cloud bill. Cut events instead. For RevenueCat, our estimate puts the switch to native beyond about $1M of tracked revenue a month, outside the horizon studied.

**LLM.** Batch, caching and quotas apply from day one. GPU self-hosting was not priced. It is not justified while LLM cost stays below a few tens of thousands of dollars a month and EU providers cover the region constraint.

**Queues and sync.** Valkey + BullMQ are added only if the Postgres queue saturates. PowerSync is added only if in-house sync costs more than its $49 a month in maintenance.

## Open decisions and risks

Five decisions must be made and recorded in DECISIONS before implementation. The first is **the EU-processed LLM provider** (Bedrock EU, OpenAI EU residency, Vertex EU or Mistral/Scaleway): no DPA was read to verify explicit acceptance of Article 9 data, nor the availability of Haiku 5.5 batch and caching on Bedrock's EU profiles. The second confirms **the from-scratch choices**: Better Auth instead of Clerk, Drizzle instead of Prisma, Bun workspaces without Nx, managed Postgres at launch. The repository documents still describe Clerk as fixed and existing apps as source references, and must be updated to match. The third checks **Better Auth's NestJS and Expo integrations** before the foundation contract relies on them. The fourth records **the Fastify adapter and Node in production**, which depart from the repository's "all Bun" picture. The fifth is **a short legal opinion on HDS**: if a pathway involving health professionals is planned, choosing now a host that also offers an HDS tier keeps that migration cheap.

The risks fall into four groups. Operationally: a single-host outage under Coolify (mitigated by keeping the database managed and the API stateless), owning auth security patches under Better Auth, Expo SDK regressions, HealthKit sync going silent after a force-quit, and Play review of the cycle permissions. Legally: a CJEU invalidation of the DPF, effective immediately, the share-alike obligation of the ODbL, and coach copy drifting toward diagnostic language, especially on the cycle. Economically: Gemini Flash prices doubling on 1 January 2027, per-event observability billing and heavy chat users. Finally, on knowledge: there is no real measurement of French token counts, DAU/MAU ratio or per-user volume, PostHog per-band prices are not confirmed on the official page, prices for Hetzner load balancers and dedicated servers, OVH and Scaleway managed offers beyond PICO were not gathered, and the cost of the DPIA and penetration test remains to be priced from quotes.

## Conclusion

Our estimates point to two levers. The first is the usage profile: product choices (briefings for active users only, short LLM outputs, chat quotas, a lean analytics event plan, aggregated health samples) change cost per MAU by a factor of 2 to 5. The second is the billing model of third-party services: per-user vendors such as Clerk and EAS Update would become the largest non-LLM lines at scale, which is why auth lives in the backend from day one. Picking the cheapest host only matters at the margin, since Hetzner is already nearly unbeatable. The stack is therefore sound, provided it is built from day one with exits in place: an internal user ID, kept native push tokens, a local copy of subscription entitlements, an LLM provider port. These are also the measures that GDPR and the stores require, so the cost of compliance and the cost of keeping vendor exits open largely overlap.

The next useful step is not another benchmark but measurement: instrument real French token counts, the DAU/MAU ratio, and per-user data and event volumes from the beta onward, then recompute this table with those numbers. The switching thresholds above can then fire on billing alerts rather than on assumptions.
